Asthma

Respiratory Emergencies: Asthma Protocol & Care

Overview

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory condition of the airways. During an asthma attack, the bronchioles experience inflammation, smooth muscle spasms (bronchospasm), and increased mucus production, leading to narrowed airways and impaired airflow.

Signs & Symptoms

  • Respiratory: Rapid breathing (tachypnea), dyspnea, wheezing, coughing, chest tightness, and difficulty exhaling due to trapped air in the chest cavity.
  • Physical Position & Effort: Tripod positioning, intercostal retractions, accessory muscle use, and inability to speak in full sentences (2–3 word dyspnea).
  • Circulatory & Skin: Rapid pulse (tachycardia), diaphoresis (sweating), pale skin, and cyanosis (bluish skin discoloration indicating hypoxia).
  • Neurological: Anxiety, restlessness, altered mental status, or lethargy (indicates impending respiratory failure).

Emergency Management & Treatment

  • Initial Actions & Priority Call: Assess Airway, Breathing, and Circulation (ABCs). Request Advanced Life Support (ALS) intercept immediately for severe distress or failure to improve.
  • Oxygen Administration: Administer high-flow supplemental oxygen via Non-Rebreather Mask (NRB) at 10–15 LPM if SpO₂ is < 94% or patient displays severe respiratory distress (e.g., 2–3 word dyspnea).
  • Bronchodilator Therapy: Assist patient with prescribed Metered-Dose Inhaler (MDI) or administer Albuterol via small-volume nebulizer driven by oxygen at 6–8 LPM per local EMS protocol.
  • Special Considerations & Medical Control: Consult Medical Control prior to Albuterol administration if the patient has a history of cardiac disease or COPD, as beta-agonists increase heart rate and myocardial oxygen demand.

Airway Management

Airway assessment

  • Airway risks: Aspiration, tongue prolapse obstruction.
  • Respiration rate: Number of breaths per minute (normal range: 12–20 breaths per minute).
  • Tidal volume: Volume of air inhaled per breath (shallow breathing is inadequate).

Positioning and opening the airway

  • Positioning: Always stand at the back of the head when managing the airway, except during CPR, where you must stay at the side.
  • Opening technique: Use the head-tilt technique for standard cases, or jaw-thrust if a spinal injury is suspected.

Suctioning

  • Suctioning technique: Ensure the airway is clear before opening it by using suction when necessary. Use a rigid catheter when the patient cannot be turned (e.g., due to a suspected spinal injury or large size), and use a soft catheter (requires an adapter) for liquid secretions. For vomit, turn the patient to the side and clear out using a gloved hand.
  • Suctioning equipment: Red tip for adults, blue tip for children; select appropriate modes on the suction device.
  • Suctioning application: Measure insertion depth against the side of the face; apply a circular motion on the way out.
  • Suctioning duration: Limit suctioning to no more than 15 seconds in adults to avoid hypoxia, though clinical judgment applies if extended suctioning is necessary to clear the airway and prevent aspiration.
  • Airway protection during suctioning: Open the patient's mouth using the cross-finger technique without tilting the head back during suctioning to prevent fluids from draining into the lungs.

Oropharyngeal airway

  • Airway adjuncts (OPA): After suctioning, insert an Oropharyngeal Airway (OPA). Size the OPA from the edge of the mouth to the earlobe (size 9/orange is typical for adults). A gag reflex indicates the patient is conscious or semi-conscious.
  • OPA insertion technique: Insert the OPA rotated 90° or 180° to avoid pushing the tongue back, then rotate it into position once past the oral cavity. Holding the tongue may help during insertion.
  • Pediatric/Head injury insertion modification: If a head injury is suspected or when treating a child, do not insert against the palate and rotate 180°.
  • Gag reflex handling: If the patient gags during insertion, remove the OPA and do not reattempt insertion unless gagging was uncertain.
  • OPA position: The OPA should sit flush with the lips after insertion. Do not force insertion; re-measure and reattempt if proper seating is not achieved.
  • OPA function: Directs airflow toward the trachea instead of the esophagus and prevents airway closure due to spasm. Note: Avoid using in head trauma as it may hit the skull.

Nasopharyngeal airway

  • NPA indications: Use a Nasopharyngeal Airway (NPA) if the patient requires ventilation support but retains an intact gag reflex (e.g., opioid overdose) or presents with trismus (locked jaw). Note: NPAs are rarely used in prehospital protocols in Israel.
  • NPA insertion technique: Size the NPA from the tip of the nose to the angle of the jaw (mandible). Apply a lubricant before insertion. Typically, insert into the right nostril, advancing gently until the flange rests flush against the nostril.
  • NPA orientation: Insert with the bevel facing toward the nasal septum. If inserting into the left nostril, keep the bevel facing the septum during insertion.

Oxygen equipment and safety

  • Oxygen flow rate: LPM on the oxygen tank should be set to 10 and increased up to 15 if necessary. This helps conserve oxygen, so start with lower rates when appropriate.
  • Shutting off oxygen: Turn off the main valve first, then the flow meter, so no residual air remains in the regulator.
  • Oxygen tank safety: Always lay the tank down flat rather than standing up. Keep away from open flames and oil. Always leave some oxygen left in the tank.
  • Oxygen duration calculation: Apps can be used to calculate remaining duration. Formula: (PSI × cylinder constant, e.g., 0.16) / LPM.

CPR and reassessment

  • Advanced airway CPR protocol: If an advanced airway is in place, deliver continuous chest compressions and give 1 breath every 6 seconds.
  • Reassessment during CPR: ONLY reassess during CPR if you see signs of life.

Shock

Understanding Medical Shock

Shock is defined as inadequate blood flow to the body's cells, leading to insufficient tissue perfusion.

The Perfusion Triangle

Proper tissue perfusion relies on the perfusion triangle, which consists of three components:

  • The Pump: The heart.
  • The Pipes: The blood vessels.
  • The Fluid: The blood.

If any one of these components fails, perfusion becomes inadequate, resulting in shock.

Types of Shock

Cardiogenic Shock (Pump Failure): Occurs when the heart muscle is unable to pump blood effectively. A common cause of cardiogenic shock is a heart attack (myocardial infarction).

Obstructive Shock: Occurs when an external obstruction prevents the heart from pumping effectively. An example is a tension pneumothorax, where air accumulates in the pleural space, causing lung collapse and exerting pressure on the heart and major blood vessels.

  • Cardiac Tamponade: Fluid accumulation in the pericardial sac compresses the heart, preventing full expansion and filling.
  • Pulmonary Embolism: A blood clot in the pulmonary artery increases right ventricular afterload, impairing heart function.

Distributive Shock (Pipe Failure): Occurs when widespread vasodilation causes a severe drop in blood pressure as blood vessel diameter expands significantly.

  • Septic Shock: A severe systemic infection triggers a widespread immune response causing systemic vasodilation. The patient's skin may appear red, flushed, and warm due to increased blood flow to the skin.
  • Neurogenic Shock: Often caused by a spinal cord injury that disrupts sympathetic nervous system communication. Without nerve signals regulating vascular tone, blood pools below the injury level, leading to a significant drop in blood pressure. Typically, skin below the injury level appears warm and flushed, while skin above remains pale and cool.
  • Anaphylactic Shock: A severe, systemic allergic reaction causing massive histamine release, widespread vasodilation, and bronchoconstriction.

Hypovolemic Shock (Fluid Failure)

Occurs from a severe loss of fluid or blood volume within the vascular system, commonly caused by severe hemorrhage, dehydration, or plasma loss from burns.

Psychogenic Shock

A temporary, sudden loss of consciousness (fainting or syncope) caused by a vasovagal reaction, resulting in temporary vasodilation and decreased blood flow to the brain.

General Treatment Guidelines

  • Positioning: Place the patient in a supine position to improve blood flow to the brain and vital organs.
  • Temperature Control: Keep the patient warm to prevent hypothermia, as body temperature regulation is often impaired.
  • Oxygen Therapy: Administer supplemental oxygen to maintain adequate tissue oxygenation.
  • Advanced Interventions: Establish IV access for fluid resuscitation and medication administration as indicated.
